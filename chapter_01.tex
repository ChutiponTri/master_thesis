Education relies heavily on assessment as a means of evaluating student learning, guiding curriculum development, and informing teaching practices. 
Among different assessment methods, written examinations stand out as a powerful tool for evaluating higher-order skills such as reasoning, problem-solving, and expressive ability. 
However, grading written exams poses significant challenges, particularly in contexts where linguistic complexity, large class sizes, and teacher workload combine to create barriers to fair and consistent evaluation. 
In Thailand, these challenges are even more pronounced due to the unique features of the Thai language and the increasing demand for efficiency in educational systems. This research addresses these issues 
by proposing an AI-assisted grading framework that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) and rule-based reasoning to improve the evaluation of Thai written exam responses.

\section{Background and Motivation}
Examinations remain one of the most critical tools in educational assessment, 
serving as a benchmark for measuring students’ comprehension, knowledge retention, 
and ability to apply concepts. Among different types of examinations, written exams 
are particularly valuable because they provide a deeper insight into students’ higher-order 
cognitive abilities, such as critical reasoning, logical explanation, problem-solving, and 
language proficiency \citep{JungX2024}. Unlike multiple-choice questions that primarily measure recognition and recall, 
written responses reveal students’ thought processes, creativity, and capacity for structured argumentation.

Despite these advantages, written exam evaluation presents significant challenges. 
Traditional manual grading is time-consuming, resource-intensive, and often inconsistent. 
Human evaluators, including teachers and teaching assistants (TAs), are susceptible to fatigue, 
emotional states, and subjective interpretation, which may lead to inconsistency in grading outcomes \citep{Mansson2016}. 
Even when grading rubrics are provided, variations in understanding and application among 
TAs may result in uneven assessment standards.

\section{Challenges in the Thai Language Context}

In the Thai educational context, the grading of written responses is further 
complicated by the unique linguistic and structural properties of the Thai 
language. Thai grammar is highly flexible, allowing for multiple valid 
sentence constructions. The language is tonal, meaning word meaning can 
change depending on pitch, and it contains numerous context-dependent 
exceptions \citep{Tongsilp2024}. Moreover, Thai writing omits spaces between words, which 
complicates segmentation and tokenization processes required for 
computational analysis.

These linguistic features make it particularly difficult for evaluators to 
interpret student responses accurately, especially when assessing reasoning, 
argumentation, or nuanced explanations. Teachers must balance linguistic 
expertise with an understanding of the subject matter and grading criteria. 
Given the open-ended nature of written responses, the risk of 
misinterpretation and grading bias is substantially higher compared to 
multiple-choice formats.

As a result, many Thai educators prefer multiple-choice examinations, which 
are quicker and easier to grade. This choice, however, comes at the expense 
of assessing higher-order skills. On average, teachers in Thailand manage 
classes of 100–400 students per subject while teaching 7–21 hours weekly 
and handling administrative, extracurricular, and pastoral 
responsibilities \citep{Goksoy2014}. Such workloads leave little room for the detailed 
evaluation of written work, leading to an overreliance on more easily 
graded exam types.

\section{Limitations of Current Assessment Practices}

Although multiple-choice exams are efficient, they fail to capture the 
depth of student reasoning, creativity, and expressive ability. 
Recent studies highlight that selected-response formats tend 
to assess surface-level understanding, whereas open-ended 
responses are better at revealing students’ conceptual 
thinking and misconceptions \citep{dossey2024automated, policar2025automated}
Written examinations, despite their higher grading workload, remain 
one of the most effective means of evaluating comprehensive 
understanding and critical thinking, especially when compared 
with highly constrained test formats 
\citep{emirtekin2025systematic, fuller2024rubric}.

In recent years, efforts to explore automated grading with 
advanced AI systems particularly large language models 
(LLMs) have expanded rapidly. These include rubric-guided 
prompting, structured evaluation protocols, and hybrid 
workflows that combine peer evaluation or partial automation 
for efficiency 
\citep{xie2024gradelikehuman, tang2025autograding}. 
However, these approaches still face limitations arising from 
the complexities of natural language and the diversity of 
student expression. For example, while rule-based or 
keyword-oriented methods can detect explicit patterns, they 
struggle with semantic nuance, paraphrasing, indirect 
reasoning, and context-dependent meaning. Even LLM-based 
approaches may exhibit inconsistent scoring behavior or rely 
on shallow reasoning shortcuts, deviating from human graders’ 
expectations 
\citep{wu2024unveilingscoring, emirtekin2025systematic}.

\section{Role of Bloom’s Taxonomy in Assessment}

Bloom’s Taxonomy provides a structured framework for 
categorizing cognitive skills, ranging from basic recall to 
higher-order reasoning and critical thinking. Despite its 
origins in mid‑20th‑century educational theory, the taxonomy 
remains highly relevant in modern assessment and AI‑enhanced 
learning research \citep{stanny2016reexamining, huber2025llms}. 
In the context of this study, Bloom’s Taxonomy plays two 
central roles.

First, it serves as a pedagogical foundation for interpreting 
the cognitive processes targeted by each exam question. This 
alignment ensures that grading criteria and model behavior 
correspond to intended learning outcomes—a principle 
emphasized in modern AI‑pedagogical models 
\citep{hmoud2024aied, lee2024flipped}.

Second, Bloom’s levels are used as a pre‑screening mechanism 
within the AI‑assisted grading framework. Expert teachers 
label each exam item according to Bloom’s categories, and 
these labels guide the selection of models and reasoning 
strategies. Recent work has shown that different Bloom levels 
may benefit from different AI strategies: for instance, tasks 
at lower cognitive levels might be handled reliably with 
general-purpose LLM prompts, while more complex analytical or 
creative tasks may require specialized prompting or hybrid 
AI–rule‑based reasoning 
\citep{scaria2024automated, luo2025enhanced}.

By integrating Bloom’s Taxonomy into the system, our approach 
achieves both pedagogical validity and technical structure, 
bridging established educational theory with AI-driven 
assessment strategies.

\section{Advances in Natural Language Processing (NLP)}

Recent breakthroughs in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and the advent 
of Large Language Models (LLMs) have opened new possibilities for automated 
exam evaluation \citep{Agarwal2021}. LLMs demonstrate strong capabilities in tasks such as 
text classification, summarization, sentiment analysis, and automated 
evaluation across multiple languages. Unlike traditional rule-based methods, 
LLMs leverage deep learning and attention mechanisms to capture contextual 
meaning, making them suitable for handling open-ended and nuanced responses.

For the Thai language, the application of LLMs introduces unique 
opportunities and challenges. While most existing LLMs are trained 
primarily on English or other widely spoken languages, recent developments 
have included multilingual models capable of handling Thai to varying 
degrees. By integrating LLMs with rule-based reasoning, it may be possible 
to design a hybrid evaluation framework that combines the flexibility and 
contextual understanding of LLMs with the consistency and transparency of 
structured rules.

The motivation behind this research is twofold. First, it 
seeks to reduce the grading burden on Thai teachers while 
enhancing fairness and consistency in evaluating written exam 
responses. Manual grading of open-ended answers often demands 
considerable time and effort, and is prone to subjectivity 
and variation across raters. By introducing an AI-assisted 
approach, the research aims to support teachers in achieving 
more objective, transparent, and efficient assessment 
practices. Second, it strives to promote the wider adoption 
of open-ended questions in Thai education, as these question 
types encourage students to demonstrate higher-order thinking 
skills such as critical reasoning, creativity, and written 
expression—competencies that are essential for lifelong 
learning but often underemphasized in traditional exam 
formats.

This thesis focuses on the development of an AI-assisted 
grading framework for Thai-language written exam questions. 
The framework integrates large language models (LLMs) with 
rule-based reasoning to combine the interpretive flexibility 
of AI with the explicit structure of human-designed rubrics. 
Through this hybrid approach, the system aims to improve 
grading accuracy, consistency, and efficiency while ensuring 
that its reasoning remains interpretable and reviewable by 
educators. The framework is designed not to replace teachers 
but to serve as an intelligent support tool that complements 
human judgment in the grading process.

The contributions of this research are multifaceted. It 
provides a comprehensive analysis of the challenges inherent 
in grading Thai written responses, identifying linguistic and 
pedagogical factors that affect evaluation consistency. It 
also presents a proof-of-concept framework that integrates 
LLMs and rule-based reasoning, offering a practical model for 
AI-assisted assessment in the Thai educational context. 
Furthermore, the study includes an empirical evaluation 
comparing the framework’s performance with that of human 
graders in terms of fairness, consistency, and 
interpretability. Finally, the findings of this research 
offer valuable insights into how AI-driven assessment tools 
can influence future educational assessment design, encourage 
the use of open-ended evaluations, and support curriculum 
development aimed at fostering analytical and expressive 
skills in students.

\section{Research Objectives}

The objectives of this research are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To explore the challenges and limitations in grading Thai written exam responses. This objective aims to investigate the underlying issues that make Thai-language written assessment difficult to evaluate consistently and fairly. It examines factors such as linguistic ambiguity, variations in student writing style, and the interpretive nature of open-ended questions. By understanding these challenges, the study seeks to identify the gaps where automation or AI assistance could meaningfully support human grading.
    \item To build a proof-of-concept AI-assisted grading framework that integrates large language models (LLMs) with rule-based reasoning. The goal is to design and implement a hybrid framework that combines the contextual understanding of LLMs with explicit rule-based logic to improve reliability and transparency in the grading process. This framework serves as an experimental prototype demonstrating how AI can analyze, interpret, and score Thai written responses in a structured and explainable manner.
    \item To compare the performance of the proposed framework with human grading in terms of fairness, consistency, and interpretability. This objective focuses on evaluating how closely the framework’s scoring aligns with human teachers’ judgments. The comparison emphasizes fairness—ensuring that the system treats responses objectively across scoring levels; consistency—maintaining stable grading patterns across different responses; and interpretability—providing clear reasoning that can be reviewed and understood by educators.
    \item To demonstrate how AI can support, rather than replace, teachers in Thai educational contexts. This research highlights the collaborative potential of AI tools in education. Rather than automating the entire grading process, the framework is envisioned as an assistive system that provides preliminary evaluations, insights, and rubric-based guidance to teachers. It aims to show how AI can reduce grading workload, enhance efficiency, and promote fairness while keeping teachers at the center of the decision-making process.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope of the Study}

This study is limited to the following scope:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The research focuses on student-level written exams under the Royal Society of Thailand, which serve as standardized Thai-language assessments. These exam questions are representative of authentic written evaluations used to assess comprehension, reasoning, and expression in the Thai context.
    \item The study covers short-answer and essay-type questions evaluated using a 0–3 scoring scale. This scale provides a manageable yet informative range for distinguishing different levels of understanding and correctness in written responses.
    \item The proposed framework employs selected large language models (LLMs) in combination with teacher-designed rubrics. The LLMs are responsible for generating initial assessments and explanations, while the rubrics provide structured criteria that guide both human and AI evaluation to ensure fairness and alignment with educational standards.
    \item All exam questions are pre-labeled according to Bloom’s Taxonomy by expert teachers. These cognitive level labels are used to guide model selection and analysis, allowing the study to explore how the framework performs across different levels of cognitive demand.
    \item The research focuses on evaluating the framework’s performance and analytical outcomes—particularly fairness, consistency, and interpretability—rather than implementing or deploying it in a live classroom environment. The study thus provides an experimental foundation and conceptual validation for potential future applications in Thai education.
\end{enumerate}
