\chapter{PROPOSED METHODOLOGY}
\label{ch:chapter_3}

This study investigates the effectiveness and reliability of 
Large Language Models (LLMs) in evaluating Thai-language 
written exam responses, using Bloom’s taxonomy as a cognitive 
framework and teacher assessments as the human baseline. The 
methodology consists of six main phases: exam collection, 
model selection, simulated response generation, AI evaluation, 
human scoring, and comparative analysis.

The process begins with collecting and labeling Thai exam 
questions, followed by selecting representative LLMs with 
diverse reasoning characteristics. Each selected model then 
generates simulated student answers, which are validated by 
expert teachers. Subsequently, the finalized test set is 
evaluated by both AI models and human raters. The resulting 
scores and explanations are compared across multiple variables 
to assess consistency, fairness, and alignment with pedagogical 
standards.

\section{Examination Data Collection and Preparation}

The dataset used in this study was obtained from the Royal 
Society of Thailand, provided through an internal academic 
collaboration facilitated by the research advisor. The source 
contained a comprehensive repository of examination materials 
across multiple disciplines, stored in a raw SQL database 
format. The dataset included both structured and unstructured 
question types, some of which contained partial metadata and 
preliminary cognitive labels.

From this database, a total of 746 examination questions were 
extracted and subsequently filtered to retain only open-ended, 
written-response items suitable for evaluating expressive 
reasoning. Two professional Thai teachers independently 
reviewed and re-labeled these questions based on Bloom’s 
taxonomy, ensuring accuracy and inter-rater consistency in 
cognitive classification.

Following this labeling process, the dataset was divided into 
two identical subsets: a non-rubric set and a rubric-assisted 
set, both containing the same questions and Bloom-level 
assignments. For the rubric-assisted set, initial rubrics were 
generated using ChatGPT to provide baseline criteria for 
evaluation, the prompt used shown in \rfig{fig:3.1_sample_prompt}.

\section{Model Selection}
\label{sec:model_selection}

In alignment with insights from the literature review, the 
study began with an initial pool of ten Large Language Models 
(LLMs): ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Grok, Claude, Gemma, OpenThaiGPT, 
Typhoon, Gemini, NemoTron, and Qwen. Each model was preliminarily 
tested using a subset of Thai written exam questions to 
evaluate its scoring behavior, reasoning transparency, and 
linguistic accuracy.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \fbox{\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{3.3_init_model}}
  \caption{Visual representation of the initial set of ten Large Language Models (LLMs) evaluated for suitability in the automated assessment experiment.}
  \label{fig:3.3_init_model}
\end{figure}

To ensure diversity and relevance, the evaluation focused on 
how differently each model interpreted and scored open-ended 
responses. Models showing overlapping scoring tendencies or 
similar reasoning styles were excluded to avoid redundancy. 
The remaining candidates were then validated by two professional 
Thai teachers, who reviewed the models’ outputs and rated 
their suitability based on accuracy, clarity of explanation, 
and pedagogical consistency.

\subsection{Model Ranking and Selection Criteria}

The model selection followed a quantitative–qualitative hybrid 
process consisting of two main components: (1) a weighted 
performance metric that captures both scoring accuracy and 
reasoning quality, and (2) a diversity criterion that ensures 
architectural and operational variety among selected models.

\subsubsection{Weighted Performance Score (WPS)}

To identify the top-performing candidates, each model was 
ranked based on its Weighted Performance Score (WPS), which 
combines objective scoring performance and qualitative reasoning 
evaluation. This approach prevents overreliance on accuracy 
alone and incorporates interpretability and reliability—factors 
critical in educational assessment.

\begin{equation}
  WPS_i = (w_{\text{Score}} \cdot S_i) + (w_{\text{Reasoning}} \cdot R_i)
\end{equation}

where:

\begin{longtable}{l p{0.7\textwidth}}
    \( i \) & Index of the model (e.g., \( i = \text{ChatGPT} \)). \\
    \( S_i \) & Normalized LLM score representing quantitative performance on benchmark or pilot scoring tasks (scaled 0–1). \\
    \( R_i \) & Normalized reasoning quality score reflecting transparency, explanation coherence, or human-rated interpretability (scaled 0–1). \\
    \( w_{\text{Score}} \) and \( w_{\text{Reasoning}} \) & Weights assigned to performance and reasoning.  
    In this study, reasoning was given slightly higher importance (\( w_{\text{Score}} = 0.4 \), \( w_{\text{Reasoning}} = 0.6 \)) due to its central role in explainable grading. \\
\end{longtable}

\subsubsection{Diversity Criterion}

Beyond numerical ranking, the final model set was refined 
to ensure conceptual and technical diversity. This step mitigates 
the risk of homogeneous error patterns and enhances ensemble 
robustness in downstream experiments. Models were grouped 
and compared across four differentiating dimensions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Commercial vs. Open-Source:}  
    To balance accessibility and capability, both proprietary (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) and open-source models (e.g., OpenThaiGPT, Typhoon) were included.

    \item \textbf{Developer Origin:}  
    Each major AI developer or foundation—OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, xAI, DeepSeek—was represented to capture diverse architectural philosophies and training pipelines.

    \item \textbf{Language Focus:}  
    Models were selected to cover both global multilingual LLMs and Thai-specific or regionally tuned models to improve cross-linguistic reliability.

    \item \textbf{Architectural Type:}  
    A mix of dense transformer models and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures (e.g., Grok, Gemini) was maintained to explore performance variation across modeling paradigms.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Final Model Selection}

Based on the combined WPS ranking and diversity validation, 
five representative models were retained for the main experiment:  
\textbf{ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Typhoon, and Grok}. Each was 
chosen for its distinct reasoning characteristics, architectural 
foundation, and demonstrated reliability in human-validated 
assessment contexts.  
This hybrid selection process ensures a balanced experimental 
setup combining accuracy, interpretability, and model diversity to 
support the study’s goal of building a fair and transparent 
AI-assisted grading framework for Thai written exams.

\section{Simulated Student Response Generation and Human Validation}

In this phase, the five selected Large Language Models 
(LLMs) from the previous stage were used to simulate student 
responses. For each examination question, the models generated 
ten variations of student answers, each scored on a scale from 
0 to 3, accompanied by a rationale explaining the reasoning 
behind the assigned score for both the rubric-assisted and 
non-rubric sets.

  \centering
  \fbox{\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{3.4_prompt_gen_student_ans}}
  \caption{Prompt used to generate student answers and scores from LLMs (Please create 10 variations of student answers for a single question, with each answer receiving a score from 0 to 3 out of a total of 3 points (in a diverse manner). The results should be presented in plain text format, with each example consisting of: 1. The student’s answer (clearly displayed), 2. The assigned score (indicated as a number from 0 to 3), 3. A clear explanation of the rationale behind the score (explained clearly, without using bullet points or headings).)}
  \label{fig:3.4_prompt_gen_student_ans}
\end{figure}

All AI-generated responses and associated scores were 
subsequently reviewed by the two professional Thai teachers. 
This human validation step ensured that the scoring rationale 
and answer quality were pedagogically sound and aligned with 
Bloom’s taxonomy levels.

The evaluation also explored the relationship between model 
performance and question characteristics. Specifically, the 
analysis aimed to identify which LLMs performed best for each 
cognitive level or question type. In cases where standard LLM 
scoring was insufficient—for example, when handling 
domain-specific language or intricate semantic 
structures—custom Python algorithms were employed. These 
algorithms, leveraging the \texttt{PyThaiNLP} framework, provided 
tailored processing to accurately interpret and score complex 
responses.

This combined AI-human approach allowed for a nuanced 
assessment of model reliability, reasoning transparency, and 
the potential need for task-specific computational adjustments.

\section{Proposed AI-Assisted Grading System}
\label{sec:prompt_template}

In this phase, the grading process was implemented using the 
previously selected LLMs. For each examination question, the 
models were prompted with the question itself, relevant rubrics 
for the rubric-assisted set, and representative sample responses 
as shown in \rfig{fig:3.5_prompt_proposed}. For 
the non-rubric set, the rubric input was omitted.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \fbox{\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{3.5_prompt_proposed}}
  \caption{Prompt template for grading a student’s response with context, rubric, and question
    provided to the LLM – Grade the student’s answer for a single question, 
    assigning a score from 0 to 3 out of 3. Present the output in plain text, including: (1) the
    numerical score (0–3), and (2) a clear explanation of the scoring rationale without bullet points
    or headings}
  \label{fig:3.5_prompt_proposed}
\end{figure}

For test grading, the best-performing LLM for each Bloom’s 
cognitive level was selected based on prior validation results. 
This approach ensured that the strengths of each model were 
leveraged according to the specific cognitive demands of the 
question.

In cases where LLMs encountered difficulties with Thai-specific 
linguistic features, custom Python modules based on the 
\texttt{PythaiNLP} framework were employed. These modules 
handled tasks such as syllable counting, word segmentation, 
and exception handling—for example, correctly segmenting 
compound words like “Phlaingam” as “Phlai-ngam.” Irregular 
forms, exceptions, and special cases were documented and 
stored in Excel for retrieval during evaluation to maintain 
consistency.

This hybrid system combining LLM scoring, rubric guidance, and 
language-specific preprocessing enabled accurate, fair, and 
contextually aware grading for Thai-language written examination 
questions.

\section{Grading Evaluation by Thai Language Teachers}

To evaluate the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed 
AI-assisted grading system, a separate test set—prepared using 
the same procedure as the experimental dataset—was independently 
graded by two professional Thai teachers. One teacher followed 
the rubric-assisted guidelines, while the other applied 
professional judgment without relying on the AI-generated rubric. 

The alignment between teacher-assigned scores and AI-generated 
scores was quantified using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient and the 
overall score agreement rate. These metrics provided a measure 
of inter-rater reliability and consistency between human and 
AI evaluation. 

Discrepancies between teacher and AI scores were further 
analyzed and categorized into three main sources:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Rubric Ambiguity}: Cases where the rubric was insufficiently specific, leading to interpretive differences in scoring.
    \item \textbf{LLM Misinterpretation}: Instances where the model misunderstood the content or reasoning required by the question.
    \item \textbf{Thai Linguistic Complexity}: Errors caused by Thai-specific language phenomena, including irregular word forms, compound words, or uncommon expressions.
\end{enumerate}

This analysis allowed for a detailed understanding of the 
limitations and strengths of both the AI-assisted system and 
human evaluators, informing subsequent improvements in rubric 
design, LLM selection, and custom language processing modules.

\subsection{Inter-Rater Reliability and Agreement Metrics}

To quantify the consistency of scoring between evaluators, two 
metrics were employed: \textbf{Cohen's Kappa} ($\kappa$) and 
\textbf{Score Agreement Rate}.

\subsubsection{Cohen's Kappa}

Cohen's Kappa measures agreement between two raters while 
correcting for agreement occurring by chance. It is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \kappa = \frac{P_o - P_e}{1 - P_e}
\end{equation}

where:  

\begin{longtable}{l p{0.8\textwidth}}
    $P_o$ & is the observed proportion of agreement between the raters. \\
    $P_e$ & is the expected proportion of agreement by chance, calculated as: \\
    {} & \vspace{-1.5em}\begin{equation}
      P_e = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_{i}^{(1)} \cdot p_{i}^{(2)}
    \end{equation}\vspace{-1.5em} \\ 
    {} & with $k$ being the number of score categories, and $p_i^{(1)}$ and $p_i^{(2)}$ representing the proportion of items assigned to category $i$ by rater 1 and rater 2, respectively. \\
\end{longtable}

The value of $\kappa$ ranges from $-1$ to $1$, where:  

\begin{tabular}{l l}
    $\kappa = 1$ & indicates perfect agreement. \\
    $\kappa = 0$ & indicates agreement equivalent to chance. \\
    $\kappa < 0$ & indicates systematic disagreement. \\
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{Score Agreement Rate}

The Score Agreement Rate (SAR) is a direct measure of agreement, 
defined as the proportion of items for which two evaluators 
assigned identical scores. Formally, it is expressed as:

\begin{equation}
    SAR = \frac{N_{\text{match}}}{N_{\text{total}}} \times 100
\end{equation}

where the variables are defined as follows:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l l}
    $N_{\text{match}}$ & Number of exact score matches between raters or between AI and human \\
    $N_{\text{total}}$ & Total number of evaluated items (questions) \\
    $SAR$ & Score Agreement Rate (\%) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This metric provides an intuitive view of raw agreement and 
serves as a complement to Cohen's Kappa by illustrating the 
frequency of exact score alignment.

Both SAR and Cohen's Kappa were applied to compare:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item AI-generated scores vs. rubric-assisted teacher scores
    \item AI-generated scores vs. teacher professional judgment scores
    \item Teacher 1 vs. Teacher 2 scores (inter-rater reliability)
\end{enumerate}

The combination of these metrics allows for a comprehensive 
assessment of the grading system’s reliability and consistency 
across evaluators.
